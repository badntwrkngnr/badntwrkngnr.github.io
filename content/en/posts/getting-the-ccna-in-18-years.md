## Introduction

I would like to begin by saying that I recently faced one of the greatest fears of my entire career: the possibility of failing the CCNA exam.

Where did that fear come from? I believe it had several origins. One of them was validation: I have been working with networks of various sizes and purposes for a long time, to be precise, 18 years. That is a considerable amount of time. Another strong reason was financial: after all, three hundred dollars do not fall from the sky, especially in my case, as I am the provider for my family. I simply could not afford to fail. To fail the exam after investing so much in books, courses, and study time, not to mention the moments I could not spend with my family, was unthinkable. With that in mind, the pressure was immense.

Returning to the subject of time, it is somewhat strange to look back and realize that I was an extremely competent generalist. I worked with virtually every type of equipment one can imagine in the telecommunications field: SDH, PDH, optical amplifiers, DWDM, CWDM, routers, switches, and firewalls.

A few years ago, I realized that something was missing: I needed to specialize in a specific area. That was when I learned about the T-shaped professional concept, which consists of being an expert in one subject while maintaining an understanding of adjacent areas. At that moment, I decided that I wanted to specialize in TCP/IP networks, leveraging all the knowledge I had accumulated in related fields.

## The Story I Would Rather Not Tell

I will not lie: it is uncomfortable to talk about mistakes we have made. I must confess that, even with all the experience and knowledge I had at the time, there came a point when I began to face significant difficulties in advancing my career due to the lack of a higher-education degree and professional certifications.

I decided to enroll in a university program and completed an associate degree in Computer Networks. Despite my years of experience, I learned a great deal. During the course, I gained a knowledge base that proved to be very important.

In parallel with my studies, I began preparing for the CCNA (Cisco Certified Network Associate) exam. The two learning paths complemented each other, and I started to understand the reasons why certain things worked the way they did. There is always room for learning.

During the degree program, however, there was a small detour. I stopped studying for the CCNA because, at a certain point, I believed I wanted to change careers. I was deeply frustrated by the need to "prove" certain things after so many years of working with networks. I misled myself for a long time. I would not call it wasted time, because I learned many things that, as fate would have it, became very useful in the not-too-distant future.

To summarize, I wanted to become a DevOps Engineer. I learned to use automation tools and concepts, which, curiously, later began to be applied in networking. That, however, is a topic for another time, it's time to return to the main subject.

## The CCNA

After realizing that I had never truly wanted to change professions, I resumed the goal of earning the CCNA. Knowing myself, I am constantly in a state of doubt and often believe I am not capable of many things. This is ironic, because I have already implemented network projects far more complex than the requirements of the CCNA. Many friends told me:

> "Pay for the exam and just take it — you will pass!"

I must confess that I wish it had been that simple, but it was not. The feeling of doubt intensified when I came to a few realizations:

> I did not know a large part of the content, especially layer 2 (Ethernet) and wireless networks.

I had to acknowledge my weaknesses and limitations, so I returned to the fundamentals. I used every resource available to learn, absorb, and implement in my homelab. As I mentioned at the beginning, failure was not an option.

It was a journey that, for the most part, was enjoyable, but it had its highs and lows. There were several moments when I considered giving up, for a number of reasons. I discovered that some things are more difficult than they appear. Out of fear of failure, I rescheduled the exam many times. Eventually, I reached a breaking point: I could no longer feel sorry for myself. I already felt like a complete fraud, after all, how could someone with so much professional experience fail to pass an entry-level exam? I needed to resolve the doubt that had consumed me for so long.

I decided to review everything during Carnival week and scheduled the exam for the following weekend. I chose to take the exam in person, so I would not have to worry about possible internet or power instability at home. The emotional burden needed some relief, as the internal pressure had reached stratospheric levels.

Before starting the exam, I was trembling, staring at the screen, stunned, completely consumed by fear. When the exam finally began, I was able to complete all the lab tasks with great agility. At that moment, it felt as if a great weight had been lifted from my shoulders, and answering the theoretical questions became easier. Only up to a point, however, because I had not been able to fully absorb everything related to wireless networks, so I decided to take the risk.

> When I finished the exam and saw on the screen that I had passed, I felt relief before I felt joy.

It took me a few days to process everything. Today, I am finally able to celebrate this achievement. The person I was a few years ago would be proud, and I would like to conclude by speaking about him.

## Conclusion

If I could go back in time and look into my own eyes, I would say:

> You will build a solid career. Despite all the difficulties, you will not give up, and you will accomplish incredible things. You are a winner — never forget that.

I would also like to take this opportunity to offer a brief reflection: look back and see everything that has been built. Do not let the world blind you to it. It happened to me, and it happens to everyone all the time. The ever-increasing demands can make you forget the person and the professional you are. Do not ignore the path you have traveled. Celebrate every achievement, no matter how small. I remind myself of this whenever I can.

If one counts all the time I have worked with networks and telecommunications, it took me 18 years to earn the CCNA. For some, that might be a source of embarrassment, but those people would not understand. Today, I feel proud of everything that has been built, and of finally having become certified.
